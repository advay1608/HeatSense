1. Executive Summary & Problem Statement

Extreme temperature variability presents a direct operational risk to coastal infrastructure, municipal power distribution, labor productivity, and public health in Mumbai, Maharashtra. Conventional climatological assessments frequently commit two critical statistical errors:

  1. Assuming daily maximum temperatures follow a simple unimodal Gaussian distribution, ignoring strong seasonal oscillations and coastal monsoon shifts.
  2. Treating extreme temperature days as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli trials, ignoring temporal persistence and multi-day heatwave clustering.

The HeatSense system evaluates 76.7 years of daily ERA5 meteorological reanalysis records for Mumbai. By combining rigorous parametric model selection, non-parametric bootstrap resampling, goodness-of-fit diagnostics, Markov chain persistence analysis, and live Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP), HeatSense provides an uncertainty-quantified characterization of thermal extremes.

Data was retrieved dynamically from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather Archive (ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis). The dataset spans 28,042 consecutive calendar days from January 1, 1950 to October 10, 2026 for Mumbai, Maharashtra.

Methodological Disclosure: Reanalysis vs. Station Sensor Data
ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis data represents a physically consistent assimilation of satellite radiances, coastal radiosondes, and surface reports across a ~0.25° grid (~25 km resolution). It represents the regional grid cell average temperature (19.0861°N, 72.8529°E, elevation 6.0m) rather than a single street-level thermometer.

Measurement Scale Note on Coefficient of Variation (CV)
Celsius is an interval temperature scale with an arbitrary zero point (0°C = 273.15 K). Therefore, presenting CV = (s / mean_C) * 100% as a physically meaningful relative variability percentage is statistically invalid, as its numerical value changes arbitrarily under affine shifts. On the true ratio Kelvin scale, relative dispersion is 0.79% (s / mean_K, where mean_K = 303.39 K). Therefore, HeatSense reports the physically valid Kelvin CV (0.79%) rather than attributing false physical meaning to Celsius CV (7.90%).

Statistical Autocorrelation & Trend Inference Limitations
Daily temperature series exhibit high serial correlation (ρ₁ = 0.9040), which artificially inflates nominal degrees of freedom and narrows daily OLS standard errors. While aggregating into complete calendar years eliminates day-to-day autocorrelation and avoids seasonal truncation bias, annual averages cannot be assumed strictly independent without qualification. Multi-year climatic oscillations (e.g., ENSO, Indian Ocean Dipole, and monsoon variability) introduce inter-annual memory, meaning regression p-values and confidence intervals must be interpreted with caution.

Meteorological Context: NWP Grid vs. IMD Mumbai Stations

The Open-Meteo operational grid cell (19.0861°N, 72.8529°E) predicts 36.8–37.6°C during active post-monsoon "October Heat". In Mumbai, October marks the immediate post-monsoon transition where clear skies and warm continental easterlies delay the cooling afternoon sea breeze. This creates strong thermal gradients: inland/suburban IMD Santacruz (typical highs 36.0–37.8°C; all-time record 38.6°C) aligns closely with the NWP grid, whereas coastal IMD Colaba (32.5–34.5°C) is heavily buffered by maritime airflow. The NWP model captures area-averaged suburban thermodynamics. HeatSense preserves authentic raw API numbers without modification.

10. Limitations & Conclusions

  • Coastal Microclimate Variation: Reanalysis data averages grid cells; high-density coastal areas and heat-island concrete clusters may experience higher micro-scale heat indices than the regional grid cell average.
  • Humidity & Wet-Bulb Considerations: Because Mumbai is a coastal city, apparent temperature and wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) are heavily influenced by relative humidity; dry-bulb maximums should be supplemented with humidity metrics for occupational health assessments.
  • Long-Term Warming: Mumbai shows a statistically significant warming trend of +0.113°C per decade across complete calendar years (1950–2025), emphasizing the importance of long-term coastal climate adaptation.
